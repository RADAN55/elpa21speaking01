From the Desk

Issue Two. The honeymoon month is over; the real year begins.

September is introductions. October is when the room settles into whatever it is going to be, and for an English learner that settling can go two ways. Either the class becomes a place with predictable routines, visible language, and a teacher who waits — or it becomes a place to disappear quietly into the back row for nine months.

This issue is about the first kind of room. Seventeen plates, each one a single idea you can carry into fourth period tomorrow: how to read silence, how to grade content without grading English, what a word problem is really asking, what to say to a family across a conference table when you do not share a language.

None of it requires a second language. All of it requires the belief that the student in front of you is already thinking in full sentences — just not yet in yours.

Every teacher is a language teacher. This is the field manual.

— R. A. Daniel, M.Ed.

The Silent Period

Listening is not nothing. It is the first reading.

Day 01 → Day 90 · hollow: silent · filled: producing · gold: first sentence
What it is
  • A normal stage, not a deficit. Newcomers may produce almost no English for weeks or months while comprehension grows daily.
  • Length varies with age, prior schooling, personality, and how safe the room feels. Older students are often quieter longer — the stakes of being wrong feel higher.
  • The student is working: mapping your routines, your gestures, the sound of instructions, who to copy.
What to do
  • Accept non‑verbal answers: point, sort, match, draw, thumbs, choose between two cards.
  • Narrate what you do in short, repeated phrases. Same words for the same actions, every day.
  • Seat them beside a patient peer, ideally one who shares a language. Ask nothing publicly until they volunteer.
  • When the first sentence comes, answer the meaning — not the grammar.
Try Tuesday

Give every direction three ways this week — say it, show it, write it on the board. Count how many times a silent student does the right thing without a word.

Four Lanes

One room. Four altitudes. The same destination.

One task, four ways · “Explain why the colonists were angry about the Stamp Act.”
Newcomer

Match 4 pictures to 4 words: tax, king, paper, angry. Circle the angry face. Say one word.

Emerging

Frame: “The colonists were angry because the king ___.” Word bank of five verbs. Two sentences.

Progressing

Frame: “Although ___, the colonists felt ___ because ___.” One paragraph with two reasons and one quote.

Challenge

No frame. Compare the Stamp Act to one modern tax dispute in a paragraph; cite the text twice.

The rules of the model
  • Same text, same standard, same objective on the board. What changes is the scaffold and the size of the output — never the thinking.
  • Lanes are not seats. A student can be Progressing in reading and Emerging in writing on the same day. Assign by task, not by label.
  • Scaffolds thin as the lane rises: pictures → word bank → frame → no frame. The horizon never moves.

The Four Domains

What ELPA21 actually measures — and what each one looks like in your room.

A typical year‑two profile: receptive domains run ahead of productive ones
Receptive — what they take in
  • Listening: following multi‑step directions, catching the main idea of a mini‑lecture, hearing the difference between “can” and “can’t.” Support: slow down, chunk, repeat key phrases verbatim.
  • Reading: vocabulary in context, text structure, inference. Support: preview headings and pictures, pre‑teach five words, read the first paragraph aloud.
Productive — what they put out
  • Speaking: usually the lowest and most public score. Support: partner before whole‑class, frames, wait time, accept accented and imperfect answers as correct answers.
  • Writing: sentence control, organization, academic word choice. Support: model the paragraph, give the frame, grade the idea first and the grammar second.
Read the profile

Ask your EL specialist for each student’s four domain scores, not just the overall level. A 4‑4‑2‑2 student needs a different Tuesday than a 3‑3‑3‑3 student.

Monday Moves

Five moves. Five minutes. Every Monday of the year.

Aug → May · navy: done · gold: October, weeks 7–10
01
Say it, show it, write it

Every direction in three channels. The board is the third channel — leave it up.

02
Wait seven seconds

Ask, then count silently. Silence is translation happening, not refusal. Most teachers wait under two.

03
Frame the sentence

“I notice ___ because ___.” One frame on the board turns a shrug into an answer.

04
Partner before public

Every question gets rehearsed with one peer first. Cold‑calling an EL without rehearsal teaches silence.

05
Word wall, used daily

Ten words for the unit, posted with a picture. Point to it. Say it. Make them say it. Grade it Friday.

This month's move

Week 7 is Move 02. Pick one class period, ask one real question, and wait the full seven seconds. Write down who answered who never had before.

The Language of Math

The problem is rarely the math. It is the eleven words wrapped around it.

A farmer has 3 times as many chickens as cows. If there are 48 animals altogether, how many cows are there?

  signal words — carry the operation         content nouns — carry the story
Signal words ELs most often misread · and the operation each one hides
Why it breaks
  • “Times as many as” reads like three separate words. Spanish speakers hear “veces” but nothing marks the comparison.
  • “If” signals a hypothetical in English. Many students read it as “first.”
  • Small words carry the weight: of, per, than, each. They are the least visible and the most tested.
What works
  • Read the problem aloud once before anyone touches a pencil. Then have students circle the numbers and box the question.
  • Post a signal‑word wall in the math room, with the operation beside each. Add to it every week.
  • Let them explain the plan in Spanish to a partner, then write the equation. The math was never the problem.

Three Tiers of Words

Where to spend your vocabulary minutes. Hint: the middle.

Tier 1 · everydaytable · walk · happy · because
Tier 2 · academic, cross‑subjectanalyze · evidence · contrast · significant
Tier 3 · subject‑specificphotosynthesis · denominator · Reconstruction
Width = share of a gen‑ed teacher's vocabulary time that should go to each tier
The argument
  • Tier 1 arrives on its own from friends, TV, and the hallway. Tier 3 you already teach — it is in the unit, bolded, on the test.
  • Tier 2 is the gap. “Evaluate,” “however,” “despite,” “approximately” appear in every class and get taught explicitly in none. ELs stall here for years.
  • A student who knows “photosynthesis” but not “process” cannot answer “Describe the process of photosynthesis.”
Ten for October

analyze · evidence · contrast · although · sequence · significant · approximately · however · determine · support

  • Post them. Use each one aloud three times a day. Require one in every written answer this month. Grade for the attempt, not the spelling.
Try Tuesday

Pull your next test. Highlight every Tier 2 word in the directions and question stems. That list is your vocabulary lesson for the week before the test.

Grading What They Know

Separate the language from the content. Then grade the content.

What the student knows · grade this
How well it was said · coach this
A science answer with four grammar errors and the right mechanism is a correct science answer
Accommodations you can give today · MTAM‑aligned
  • Extended time — the single most effective and most under‑used accommodation.
  • Bilingual word‑to‑word dictionary (no definitions). Allowed on MAAP; allow it on yours.
  • Directions read aloud and clarified in plain English. Test items read aloud on math and science, not on ELA reading passages.
  • Small‑group or separate setting. Fewer items per page. Answers written directly in the test booklet.
Grading practices that hold up
  • Write two rubric lines: content and language. Weight content. Report both so growth in language is visible too.
  • Accept answers in drawings, labeled diagrams, or Spanish with a partner’s English gloss for newcomers in the first semester.
  • Never a zero for silence. An un‑attempted item for an EL is missing data, not a failing score. Re‑ask it another way.
  • The LSP is the contract. If it says extended time, a grade given without it is not defensible.
The one question

Before you enter a grade for an English learner, ask: if this student had written it perfectly in Spanish, what grade would the idea earn? Start there.

The Compliance Desk

October, plotted. Thirty‑one days, five Mondays, one date that matters.

On the desk this month
  • Every EL has a current LSP on file and every teacher of record has read it and signed that they did.
  • Accommodations are living in the gradebook and the test settings, not just the cum folder.
  • First‑quarter grades checked against the §15 EL progress pool — a failing EL in October is a May problem you can still fix.
  • Parent notification sent in a language the family can read. Logged. Dated. Copied to the folder.
The framework, in one breath
  • Title III funds the services. EEOA (1974) requires “appropriate action” to overcome language barriers.
  • Castañeda (1981) tests that action three ways: sound theory, adequate resources, actual results.
  • ELPA21 is the annual measure; MS §15 puts EL progress into the school’s accountability grade. Your gradebook is evidence.

Ground rules
  • Never use the student as the interpreter for their own conference. Ask the office for an interpreter or a phone line; the district is required to provide one.
  • Lead with a strength, in detail. Families of ELs often hear only problems. Bring one piece of work to hand them.
  • Ask, then wait. The second question — “What do you want me to know?” — is where the useful information lives.

The First Thirty Days

A newcomer’s month, day by day. What the room owes them, and when.

Week one · safety
  • A seat near the front and beside a bilingual buddy. A printed schedule with room numbers and teacher photos.
  • Twenty survival words and phrases on a card: bathroom, water, help, I don’t understand, please repeat, where is.
  • No grades. Presence, participation by pointing, and one smile a period.
Weeks two–three · routine
  • Every class has one predictable routine the student can do without instructions: bell work, exit ticket, a labeled diagram.
  • First written lines: copying, then a frame. First spoken answer: rehearsed with the buddy, volunteered, never demanded.
Week four · evidence
  • The first graded task uses the Newcomer lane: pictures, word bank, one sentence. It goes in the folder as a baseline you will compare against in December.
  • By day 30 the EL specialist should have the screener score, the LSP drafted, and every teacher’s signature. If any of those is missing, ask.

Grammar Beats

Ritmos de gramática. A rule you can clap is a rule you keep.

October session · Grade 10 · English II · past vs. progressive

Walk‑ed, talk‑ed, jump‑ed — past.

Walk‑ing, talk‑ing, jump‑ing — now.

Clap the clave. Say the rule. Keep the time.

Why rhythm
  • Inflections (‑s, ‑ed, ‑ing) are unstressed in English and nearly inaudible to a Spanish‑dominant ear. Putting them on the beat makes them loud.
  • A three‑minute clap‑and‑chant at the start of class is rehearsal for the whole period’s writing.
Run the session
  • 1. Teacher claps the clave; class echoes. 2. Add the words on the beat. 3. Students write three sentences using the pattern. 4. Swap papers; circle every inflection.
  • Recorded albums exist for the MAAP‑tested grades (3–8, English II) in The Blueprint / El Plano.

Readable: The Fair

One text, two lanes, three questions. Print this page.

Emerging · ELPA21 1–2 · ~80 words

Every October, the Mississippi State Fair comes to Jackson. It is big. There are rides, lights, and music. You can smell fried food and sweet funnel cakes. Farmers bring cows, goats, and pigs to show. Students from many schools come on buses. Some students speak English. Some speak Spanish. At the fair, everyone is loud and happy. The fair lasts about twelve days. Then the lights go out until next year.

Progressing · ELPA21 3–4 · ~150 words

Each October the Mississippi State Fair transforms the fairgrounds in Jackson into a small city of lights. For roughly twelve days, visitors arrive from every county to ride the Ferris wheel, watch livestock competitions, and eat food that exists nowhere else in the calendar. Although the rides draw the crowds, the fair began as an agricultural event, and it still is one: young farmers spend months raising a calf or a goat to show in the ring, and a blue ribbon can mean a scholarship. Schools across the state bring students by the busload. In the crowd you hear English, Spanish, and Vietnamese, sometimes in the same conversation. When the last night ends, the lights are packed into trucks, and the fairgrounds return to quiet until the following fall.

Three questions · same questions, both lanes
1
When does the fair happen, and where?
Emerging: circle the month and the city in the text. Progressing: answer in one full sentence.
2
Why did the fair begin? Find the sentence that tells you.
Emerging frame: “The fair began as ___.” Progressing: explain what “still is one” refers to.
3
The text says the crowd speaks many languages. Why might the writer include that?
Emerging: choose — a) it is loud  b) many kinds of people come  c) the food is good. Progressing: two sentences with a reason.
Tier 2 words to pre‑teach: transform · roughly · although · competition · exists · return

Ask the Specialist

Four questions from the hallway, answered before the bell.

“He speaks English fine at lunch. Why is he still an EL?”

Playground English arrives in one to two years. Academic English — the language of textbooks, tests, and essays — takes five to seven. Social fluency is real and it is not the same skill. ELPA21 measures the second one, and the exit criteria are set by the state, not by how he sounds in the cafeteria.

“Can I just let her use Google Translate?”

For a newcomer reading directions, yes, and it beats sitting lost. For producing her own writing, use it as a bridge, not a destination: she drafts in Spanish, translates, then rewrites the translation in her own words with a frame. Grade the rewrite. The tool should be visibly doing less by December.

“She never turns anything in. Is that a language problem or a behavior problem?”

Before you decide, check three things: can she read the assignment page, does she know when it is due, and has anyone shown her one finished example. Silence and non‑completion are what an unclear task looks like from the outside. Then, if all three are in place, treat it as you would for any student.

“I have 28 kids and two ELs. How much am I supposed to do?”

Nothing separate. The moves in this issue — wait time, frames, three‑channel directions, word walls — are good teaching for all 28. The ELs are the students for whom it stops being optional. Start with one move and keep it for a month.
